Aevo + Vera · Safety architecture

Two AI roles.
One unbreakable rule.

Aevo talks with patients. Vera checks the work and routes it. Neither one is allowed to make a clinical decision — and nothing clinical reaches a patient without a licensed human approving it first.

The rule: AI classifies, drafts, and organizes. Humans decide. This is enforced in the workflow's architecture — the send action for clinical guidance does not exist without a pharmacist approval step in front of it.
The division of labor

Aevo faces the patient. Vera faces the record.

Splitting the system into two named roles isn't branding — it's separation of concerns. The AI that talks to a vulnerable patient should never be the same component that decides what's safe to say.

Aevo
Access · Equity · Voice · Outcomes

The patient-facing layer. Designed for plain language on any phone, starting with SMS in English and Spanish.

  • Receives patient messages and asks clarifying questions in plain language
  • Preserves the patient's original words verbatim — the patient's voice is data, not noise
  • Drafts supportive, non-clinical responses from pharmacist-approved templates
  • Surfaces 911 guidance immediately when red-flag language is detected
  • Roadmap: additional languages and voice channels as pilots define the need
Vera
Validate · Educate · Reach · Advocate

The clinical-side layer. Turns what patients say into what care teams need — and enforces the boundaries.

  • Classifies each message with deterministic, inspectable rules — not a black box
  • Translates patient language into a structured pharmacist review brief
  • Tags barrier signals: cost, side effect, transportation, food, confusion
  • Holds every clinical draft in the approval queue until a pharmacist releases it
  • Writes the audit trail: what was received, classified, drafted, approved, and sent

The boundaries

Designed to do less than it could.

What the system is designed to do

  • Listen in the patient's own words and keep those words intact
  • Ask clarifying questions from approved, pharmacist-reviewed scripts
  • Classify concerns into tiers with auditable rules
  • Prepare structured review briefs for pharmacists
  • Capture barrier signals — cost, access, side effects — as structured data
  • Provide immediate 911 guidance on red-flag language
  • Log every step for audit

What it will never do

  • Diagnose a condition — it informs, flags, and defers
  • Tell a patient to stop or change a medication — that requires a licensed provider
  • Tell a patient a concern is "not serious" — it never minimizes
  • Send clinical guidance a pharmacist hasn't approved
  • Message a patient who hasn't opted in, or ignore a STOP
  • Handle PHI outside BAA-covered, HIPAA-eligible infrastructure
  • Replace the judgment of the humans it serves
Why it's built this way

Three deliberate engineering choices.

Deterministic before generative The pilot triage engine uses inspectable rules instead of a language model. In a clinical-adjacent workflow, "we can show you exactly why it routed that way" beats a confidence score. Generative drafting arrives later — behind the same human-approval gate, never in front of it.
Human approval is structural The approval queue isn't a policy that could be toggled off under pressure. The workflow has no path from AI draft to patient without a pharmacist action in between. Removing the gate would require rebuilding the system, not reconfiguring it.
The patient's words are the product Most systems normalize patient language away. Orivo preserves it: "I feel weird when I stand up" reaches the pharmacist alongside the structured brief, because the original phrasing carries clinical signal that categories lose.
